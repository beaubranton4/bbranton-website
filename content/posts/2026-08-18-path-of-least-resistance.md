# August 18, 2026

I'm really starting to feel the impacts of using AI on my brain. Whenever I have to make any sort of decision, or a hard question comes up, my automatic default is just to say, "what would ChatGPT do?" and ask it the question — even something as simple as "should I make this red or blue?" I catch myself offloading the simplest things, things that would take one second. Sometimes I even find myself preferring to ask ChatGPT, even though I know it'll give me a 60-second-long response, versus just doing the thinking myself. What's eight plus four plus three plus nine? That's too much, let me ask ChatGPT. It's the natural law of our brains taking the path of least resistance. I'm not sure if it's a good thing or a bad thing — I don't want to make a claim one way or the other. But what I do know is that what the human brain spends time thinking about is going to be a lot different today and in the future than what it used to spend time thinking about pre-AI, pre-ChatGPT. It's these tools and technologies that are really shaping how our brains work and what brain skills we value as a society.

For example, Excel — super powerful software for analytical, logical thinkers, people who can do a bit of math. Not every brain is shaped like that. Not every brain can think in formulas and columns and tables and equations. Of course it can be trained, but there are going to be people who are better at that than others, and those people become more valued over time throughout companies and organizations that need to stay organized, that need analysis, that need data. Now that all the tools can be controlled through spoken word, what becomes valuable? Is it the people who can express their thoughts, who can be eloquent and describe what they need and want? Is it the people with experience, the people who know what they want? I know for certain that the pure action of performing tasks in Excel is not as valuable anymore. The ability to *think* like someone who works in Excel — maybe that's still valuable. There are going to be tools that can be controlled via natural language much better than others. I still don't think beautiful movie-quality video can ever be fully recreated via prompting, whereas it's already been proven that things like code and text can be done relatively easily that way.

I relate all that back to how my own brain has been working. I do find that I've been taking the path of least resistance — not only when working with AI, but in life, in projects, in the side hustle. The problem is that taking the path of least resistance often leads to a lack of growth. Take the opposite case: salmon swimming upriver. They are obviously not taking the path of least resistance, and they grow bigger and stronger because of it — they're swimming up waterfalls. How hard is that? You do the hard work, you have an easy life. You do the easy work, you have a hard life. That's what I'm starting to get worried about as I continue to use these AI tools and try to build something digital — I just keep taking the path of least resistance.

And in some ways, maybe that's the key. The path of least resistance is often what's most fun — what you enjoy doing, what you love, what you're good at. Why spend your whole life doing something you don't like and you're bad at? So it's a fine balance I'm trying to navigate on my own: am I just not working hard enough, or am I just not working on the right thing? I think the guiding principle is just to do what I like, do what I love, do what interests me in the moment, follow my curiosity, and the stars will align themselves.
